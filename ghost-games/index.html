Ghost Games · Europe's top five leagues

Take the fans away and the referee stops favouring the home team.

Colin Spelman · 2,234 matches played without crowds, against 14,056 with them · November 2026

Halloween just passed, and I spent it with ghosts of my own: the 2,234 top-flight matches Europe played in empty stadiums. Silent stands, echoing shouts, nobody whistling the referee. Here is what that did to home advantage.

For about 13 months in 2020 and 2021, top-flight football in England, Spain, Italy, Germany and France was played in empty stadiums. That is the closest the sport will get to a controlled experiment on what crowds do. The answer: the referee's home lean vanished almost completely, home teams lost over a third of their shots-on-target edge, and 63% of home advantage in points went with it. When fans came back, so did the referee's lean.

Look at the cards. Home teams were booked about as often as before (1.91 to 1.93 a match). Away teams were booked less (2.17 to 1.95). With fans, each away foul was 11% more likely than a home foul to draw a yellow. Without them the gap fell to 2%. The fouls flipped too: with fans, referees called more fouls on the away side; in empty stadiums they called slightly more on the home side.

What disappeared

Two stories, two different sizes

The referee story is clean

The card edge sat between 0.2 and 0.35 a match for a decade, fell to nearly zero (0.01) in empty stadiums, and was back to 0.27 by 2022-23 onwards. About 61% of the drop came from referees booking away players less often per foul, and 35% from calling fewer fouls on them in the first place.

The points story is bigger than the shots

Against that trend, empty stadiums cut the home points edge by 0.29 a match, from 0.46 to 0.17 (−63%). Over a 38-game season, that is roughly 3 fewer points at home and 3 more away for a typical team: the totals barely move, the split between home and away does.

The shooting edge explains only part of it. With fans, each extra shot on target was worth about 0.27 points, so the smaller shooting edge accounts for 29% of the points drop. Red cards account for almost none. The other 70% came from something the box score doesn't capture: finishing, game management, or decisions this data can't see, like penalties and added time.

Did it come back?

Mostly, yes. From 2022-23 to 2025-26 the points edge averaged 0.38 a match, below the 0.49 of 2012-2020. Most of that is the decline that was already under way: projecting the pre-2020 trend forward and adjusting for team strength, the post-COVID seasons sit 0.04 below it (95% interval −0.11 to +0.02), which is within the noise. The yellow-card edge came back fully: 0.27 a match, against 0.27 before. Put the fans back and the referee's lean comes back with them.

The card result holds in every version, including a comparison inside the 2019-20 season alone (same teams, same squads, before and after the restart). The points result is steady across specifications but not significant inside 2019-20 alone, where the post-restart sample is small (about 400 matches). The placebo, which pretends crowds vanished in the same calendar window three years earlier, finds nothing, as it should.

Objections, answered before you ask

"Lots of things changed in 2020, not just the crowds."

True: five substitutes, congested fixtures, a summer restart. None of them should make a referee book away players less than home players, which is the cleanest result here. For points, the 2019-20 within-season comparison holds squads and the season fixed, and the trend control stops the long-run decline being counted as a crowd effect.

"VAR arrived around the same time."

VAR was in place in every league before the empty period (Serie A and the Bundesliga from 2017-18, La Liga and Ligue 1 from 2018-19, the Premier League from 2019-20), so it is in the "with fans" baseline for the comparisons that matter, and it does not review ordinary yellow cards.

"Some empty games had a few thousand fans."

276 matches fell in windows with small capped crowds. Dropping them makes both results slightly larger, which is what you'd expect if crowds are the cause.

"Fouls and cards are the referee's call, not the players' behaviour."

Partly both. That is why the card edge is split into fouls called and cards per foul: the cards-per-foul part is the referee's discretion, and it carries 61% of the change.

"What about penalties?"

Not in this dataset. They are the obvious next layer and probably part of the unexplained points drop.

Data and method

Data: 25,241 league matches, Premier League, La Liga, Serie A, Bundesliga and Ligue 1, 2012-13 to 2025-26 (football-data.co.uk via the datasets/football-datasets mirror): results, shots, shots on target, fouls, yellow and red cards, and referee names for the Premier League. 2021-22 and the 2020 suspension window are excluded (1,869 matches) because crowds returned unevenly.

Model: each outcome is a home-minus-away gap for one match, regressed on an empty-stadium indicator, league fixed effects, the pre-match Elo gap (computed without any home term) and a linear season trend. Heteroskedasticity-consistent (HC1) standard errors; team-season clustering as a check. The main estimates rebuild from the public data with one command (replicate.py on GitHub).
